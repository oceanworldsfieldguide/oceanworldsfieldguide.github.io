Module 9: Team exercise: In-situ instrument package for a Ganymede mission
Authors: M.Chiviri, M.J.Malaska, J.Hofgartner

Introduction – Team Exercise
Unlike the preceding modules in this handbook, Module 9 describes the planning for and outcomes from a Team Exercise that was developed for participants in the in person NOW retreat held on Catalina Island in late Summer, 2023. The goal of the team exercise was for the participants to apply all of the information and knowledge they had gained from the preceding modules (1-8) and harness them to plan for NASA mission that would search for extant life on Ganymede. The participants were divided into eight teams which, prior to the workshop, had been carefully selected and balanced based on the skills and experience levels of the participants. We tried to ensure that every team included at least one member with expertise/experience in each of the following fields: astrobiology, mission planning, instrumentation, chemistry, microbiology, and planetary geology. The teams’ members were identified early in the workshop to provide the opportunity for familiarization with each other, and the mission scenario was also presented during the initial session of the retreat. Over the ensuing days, the teams had specific meeting times set aside so that they could plan and discuss not only the instrument packages they might select, but also the specific signals that they would choose to convince the broader scientific community that they had detected extant life. At the end of the workshop, the different teams presented a concise set of slides with their proposed instrument packages and their selections on what should constitute convincing data for the confirmation of extant life, followed by audience questions and feedback. The different teams’ results, in aggregate, provided a snapshot of community knowledge and the current state-of-the-art of astrobiology instrumentation within the field of Ocean Worlds investigaions. It also provided valuable information on strategies and needs to develop the next generation of astrobiological instrumentation.

Level 1: Foundational Concepts and Basic Information

1.1 The Team Exercise Scenario – Ganymede mission.
Each team was presented with the same challenge:

“Recent discoveries suggest that there might be a location on Ganymede with the best chance to find extant life. A new surface mission has been planned that will target that location. The mission has a drill that will deliver an unconsolidated icy sample from appropriate depth to your lander.

Your Team is tasked with selecting an instrument suite that will provide the best evidence for detecting extant life. Your team goal is to create an instrument suite composed of no more than 3 instruments from past or current flight missions (Perseverance, Curiosity), or very advanced planned missions (Rosalind Franklin, Dragonfly, and Europa lander) but not any advancements beyond the described or strawman payloads for those missions. (The instrument list is detailed in the slide below). Your team is also tasked with presenting the raw data (peaks, signals, graphs, etc.) from your chosen instrument package that will convincingly demonstrate to the scientific community that you have found extant life.
Other constraints are that instrument chaining (advanced integration beyond what has already flown) is not allowed, but co-boresighting to the same sample is allowed. Another key constraint is that optical instruments are not allowed to look at scales larger than 5x5 pixels at any resolution (thus, no smiley faces, no diatom morphologies, etc.). “

Instrument list (broken down by previous target body and mission):

“Your team’s output will be a 5 minute presentation to make your case and sell your instrument suite and data output. You will have a maximum of 5 slides. Key elements of that slide package are :

  • Selected 3 instrument suite

  • Graphs or plots of raw signals that would provide the best evidence for extant life detection. (Example: if your team claims to find ATP with a Raman spectrometer, your team needs to show the signals in the expected spectrum that support claim for ATP).

  • Describe data interpretation: How do you know what you know?

  • Detail possible confounders, alternative explanations to extant life

As a bonus, you can include an extra slide: If your team was allowed one more instrument, or a follow-up mission, detail what additional instrument could be used to provide further proof or break degeneracies.”

This was the challenge set out to all the teams. Timing of the workflow was as described below.

Level 2: Detailed Exploration and Advanced Understanding
Teams were established and their challenge presented on Day 1 of the retreat which included scheduled time on following days for each team to meet and discuss ideas amongst themselves as the rest of the workshopunfolded and an increasing breadth of information was provided. In addition to the scheduled “Module 9” work periods, the teams were also free to gather and discuss informally throughout the unscheduled portions of the workshop, over dinner, during free time, etc. There were no explicit rules regarding communication between teams. (But in a true competitive environment, this would be limited.)

The formal schedule of the exercise was as follows:
Workshop Day 1 (During break, 15 min): Mission and exercise presented. Teams announced.
Workshop Day 1 to Workshop Day 3: Formal and informal meeting time available for Module 9 work
Workshop Day 3:
Exercise Part A (1h): Teams finalize 3 slide package of proposed instruments and data presentation
Exercise Part B (1.5 h): Teams present their instrument suite and underlying data to entire workshop. Open critique and discussion by audience (10 min open discussion and synopsis).
Exercise Part C: Post-presentation discussion
- What were the common themes from each of the teams?
- Did a “best/preferred” solution emerge?
- What critical technology gaps were identified?

Level 3: Technical Insights and Case Studies

Synthesis & Future Directions

The majority of the teams selected a state-of-the art mass spectrometer [MS] system for chemical analysis (DraMS or MOMA).

Surprisingly, nearly half of the teams also selected the Viking mission “Labelled-Release” experiment - albeit with suggested improvements and modifications..

Half of the teams also selected the Phoenix mission MECA suite (most for the wet chemistry laboratory[WCL] but one team also selected the atomic force microscope [AFM] component). Interestingly, while Phoenix was not an astrobiology mission, it provided a testbed for a key astrobiological instrument. This reflected a paraphrased comment from an earlier presentation by Alfonso Davila: “Do not launch an astrobiology mission, you will only be disappointed in the (ambiguous) results.”

If an additional instrument were allowed as part of the package, given a choice between adding an imaging device or some other type of instrument, half of the teams selected a different instrument type.

Key takeaways and knowledge gaps identified from the exercise.

There is a need to:

  • Advance the state-of-the-art of chemical analysis, specifically planetary [separation, or even multistage] mass spectrometry. For future instrument development, key improvements should be pursued in all of:

  • derivatization

  • separation resolution

  • mass resolutio

  • multi-stage capabilities.

  • Update and modernize the Viking mission’s “Labelled Release” experiment for future missions.

  • Enable opportunities to prove future astrobiology instrumentation by selecting non-biological applications.

  • Examine complementary non-imaging instruments for development and instrument suite integration.

Concluding Remarks.

Overall this was a fun and useful, thought-provoking exercise. Looking forward, it could be presented to an advanced graduate student course as well as at future workshops. It could equally provide an interesting topic for discussion less formally, around a dinner table or campfire at a conference / retreat.

With additional time, future iterations could allow designated teams to review and critique other teams’ proposals, with each original team then having time to respond to those critiques. This would likely require a full week-long workshop (and additional time commitment), but in a different context, it could be adapted into a useful student exercise - one that would include simulating a formal review process.
